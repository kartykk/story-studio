Complicit
=========

Logline: A corrupt Mumbai cop investigating a brutal murder must expose his own precinct or become the next victim buried by its secrets.
Theme: Complicity is not safety — the systems we corrupt to protect ourselves will ultimately consume us.

# Chapter 1: Paper in a Dead Man's Throat

The tube light above the evidence room had been dying for eleven days. Avinash had counted. It stuttered now in a rhythm he had memorized — three pulses of sickly white, then a darkness that lasted exactly long enough for the afterimage to bloom green behind his eyelids, then three pulses again. He worked through the flickers without looking up, the way a man walks a staircase in his own home. No need for light when your hands know the sin by touch.

The seizure report was a simple job. Two kilos of charas logged from a Bandra raid six weeks ago. The actual haul had been closer to five, but Avinash didn't concern himself with where the difference went. His task was narrower: the timestamp on the chain-of-custody form read 02:47, and it needed to read 03:12 — twenty-five minutes added to align with a vehicle checkpoint log that Senior Inspector Deshpande had already submitted to the magistrate's office. Avinash uncapped a Cello Gripper pen, the same brand used by the booking constable, and wrote the new time in a hand indistinguishable from the original. He had practiced Constable Mane's handwriting four years ago, sitting in this same chair. Mane wrote his threes like stunted eights. The sevens had no crossbar. These were the things Avinash knew about the men he worked with — not their children's names or their wives' birthdays, but the specific failures of their penmanship.

He blew on the ink. Not necessary. Habit.

The evidence room smelled of mildew, mouse droppings, and the particular staleness of paper that has absorbed decades of coastal humidity. Filing cabinets lined the walls in mismatched rows — olive green, gunmetal grey, one inexplicable beige unit that everyone called the *coffin* because its bottom drawer jammed shut and never opened. The room had no windows. The floor was poured concrete that sweated in the pre-monsoon months, slick enough that someone had laid flattened cardboard boxes as a path from the door to the main work table. The cardboard was soft as skin now, darkened with footprints.

Avinash slid the doctored report into its folder and the folder into the second cabinet from the door, third shelf, between the Kamath assault case and a missing-persons file for a Nepali dishwasher no one was looking for. His fingers brushed the adjacent shelf and stopped.

A water stain had warped the label on the folder beneath. But not enough. Not nearly enough to hide the name.

*Qureshi, Irfan M. — FIR 417/2017.*

He did not open it. He knew every page. He knew the weight of it — slightly heavier than it should be because he had added three sheets of fabricated witness testimony on the night of the arrest, typing them on the station's Lenovo desktop while Irfan sat handcuffed to a bench eighteen feet away, a man so frightened he had wet himself and was trying to hide the stain with his hands. Avinash remembered the sound of Irfan's breathing — not sobbing, not pleading, just the metronomic rasp of a man whose body was still functioning while his understanding of the world collapsed. Avinash had heard that sound many times since in rooms where it did not exist.

He closed the drawer. The metal caught, then slid home.

A chai appeared at the edge of the table. Deshpande's thick hand withdrew without ceremony — the cup placed the way a coin is placed in a temple box, a transaction so routine it required no prayer. Avinash heard Deshpande's chappals slap the wet cardboard, pause at the doorway.

"Patil hasn't called in." Deshpande's voice carried the particular flatness of a man noting a delayed train — annoyance, not alarm. "Second shift. Find out."

The chappals slapped away.

Avinash drank the chai. Tepid, too much sugar. He carried the cup to the main floor.

---

The D.N. Nagar precinct at 5 a.m. was a body pretending to sleep. Ceiling fans carved uneven circles through air that tasted of old sweat and phenyl. Two constables dozed on a bench near the lockup, their lathis propped between them like the legs of a shared cot. The duty officer, a man named Bhosle, was watching a Tamil film on his phone with the brightness turned down and one earpiece in, the other ear trained on the static-laced police radio — a skill set more finely honed than anything in his training file. Behind the scarred wooden counter, a woman in a torn nightgown waited on the complaint bench with the glazed patience of someone who had been told to sit hours ago and understood that the word *sit* meant *disappear*.

Avinash crossed to Bhosle's desk with a petty theft report that needed a discrepancy smoothed — a shopkeeper's claimed losses didn't match the inventory photographs, a gap that would bother the magistrate if anyone was paying attention, which no one was, which was the point. He adjusted two figures with the Cello Gripper, initialed the margin.

Constable Pawar leaned over, eighteen months on the job, still stupid enough to grin.

"Kadam sir, you make it look like nothing." Pawar's teeth were stained with paan. "I tell you, as long as we keep each other's secrets in this station, we are safe. Safer than a bank."

Avinash capped the pen. He looked at Pawar for a moment that lasted one beat too long — long enough for the boy's grin to thin at the edges, long enough for a question to form that Pawar would never ask and Avinash would never answer. Then Avinash placed the pen in his shirt pocket and walked toward the exit.

Outside, the sky was the color of a bruise finding its edges. Across the street, the Irani chai stall was already lit, its fluorescent tube drawing the first auto-drivers and newspaper vendors into orbit. A young woman sat at the stall's far corner, her back against the wall, a glass of cutting chai untouched before her. She was watching the precinct entrance with the unhurried attention of someone counting inventory — who entered, when, in what order, with what weight on their shoulders. She wore a plain kurta the color of dry earth and her dupatta was pulled forward enough to shadow her face without appearing to hide it.

Avinash did not notice her. He was not the kind to scan tea stalls for threats — he carried his threats internally, in a locked drawer with a water-stained label.

He lit a cigarette and waited for the call he already expected.

---

The textile mill was a skeleton.

Three floors of exposed rebar and shattered looms, the roof peeled back by developers who had run out of money or permits or both. Lower Parel was full of such carcasses — the old cotton mills gutted and half-converted into luxury towers, the construction halted at various stages of ambition. This one had no name anymore. The signboard had been stolen for its metal. What remained was a concrete shell colonized by rats, rainwater, and the particular darkness that gathers in buildings people have stopped caring about.

Avinash ducked under the police tape at 6:23 a.m. The humidity was a living thing — it pressed against his face like a wet cloth, filled his lungs with the taste of rust and standing water. Two patrol constables stood near the entrance, smoking with the determined normalcy of men who had seen something inside that they were already rehearsing how to forget.

Sub-Inspector Manoj Patil lay on the ground floor, between a collapsed conveyor frame and a wall tagged with a mobile number for *Vastu consultation*. He was on his back. His service shirt was unbuttoned to the sternum, as though someone had needed access to his chest, but there were no chest wounds. The wound was at his throat — a single deep cut, left to right, the kind made by someone standing behind and pulling. The blood had pooled and begun to thicken in the heat, dark as molasses, colonized already by flies whose sound filled the space like a low mechanical hum.

Avinash crouched.

Patil's mouth was open. Not the slack openness of death — forced open, jaw distended, the lips split at the corners. Something had been pushed inside. A wad of material, packed tight, stained with saliva and blood. Paper.

Avinash pulled a latex glove from his pocket, stretched it over his left hand. He did not touch the body. He leaned closer.

Shredded paper. Strips no wider than a thumbnail, compressed into a mass the size of a man's fist and forced past the teeth with enough pressure to crack the lower left canine. Avinash could see ink on the visible strips — typed text, columnar formatting, the faded blue of government-issue carbon copy.

He knew that formatting. He had filled in hundreds of those columns.

FIR case-filing codes. Internal numbering. The specific layout used by D.N. Nagar between 2016 and 2019, before the new digital system was half-heartedly adopted.

"Gang hit," Deshpande said behind him. Avinash hadn't heard him arrive. Deshpande stood with his hands in his pockets, surveying Patil's body with the expression of a man assessing water damage to a rental property. "Bhandari crew. Patil owed them for the Malad thing. Clean it up. Rajan Shinde — he's in Byculla, already has two cases pending. Pin it, file it, I'll talk to the magistrate."

Avinash looked at the paper in Patil's throat. At the filing codes visible between blood and saliva.

"I'll need the fragments analyzed."

"You'll need to close this in forty-eight hours," Deshpande said. "Patil was one of ours. People will ask questions. Give them answers before they start asking the wrong ones." He adjusted his belt — the gesture he used instead of making threats. "Rajan Shinde, Avinash. Dead man's debts, dead man's enemies. Simple."

"That's one version."

Deshpande's eyes narrowed. A silence opened between them that had the texture of a door being tested. Then Deshpande turned and walked back toward the tape line, pulling out his phone, already managing the next layer.

Avinash stayed crouched. He photographed the paper with his own phone, angling for the clearest strips. On three fragments he could make out sequential case numbers. The series began with 4-1, which placed them in the 2017 fiscal year. One strip showed a partial name in the complainant column — the ink was smeared, but the descenders suggested a Q or a G, and the spacing was wrong for any Marathi name in the registry.

*Qureshi.*

The tube light in the evidence room. The drawer that jammed and then didn't. The breathing of a man handcuffed to a bench.

Seven years ago.

Avinash stood. His knees cracked. The sound was louder than it should have been, or the mill was quieter, or both.

---

He drove two blocks from the mill and pulled the car to the shoulder on Senapati Bapat Marg. Rain had started — not the monsoon itself but its emissary, fat intermittent drops that struck the windshield like thrown gravel. He killed the engine. Turned off the headlights.

The obvious move was Rajan Shinde. Pin it, file it, collect the chai from Deshpande's hand tomorrow morning. Patil's murder would join the cabinet, another folder in the correct slot, another set of numbers that added up because someone had made them add up. Safe.

*Safe as a bank.*

But the paper in Patil's mouth was from the locked room. The room Avinash had sealed himself in 2019 — old case files moved to dead storage during the digitization push, boxed and shelved in the basement corridor behind the generator. No one went there. No one had reason to. Unless someone had gone there first, and taken the files, and shredded them into strips, and packed them into the throat of a man whose job it was to keep secrets.

A message. Not for Patil. Patil was the envelope.

Avinash pulled out his phone and opened Patil's recent duty logs — he had access, every sub-inspector's movements were tracked through a shared GPS app that no one admitted was monitored. He scrolled back through the last three weeks. Station. Court. Station. Home. Station. And then, six times in fourteen days, a location pin that dropped in Dharavi — the same coordinates each time, deep in the scrap-market lanes near the Mahim junction, an area with no active cases assigned to Patil or anyone else in D.N. Nagar.

Six visits. No filed reports. No informant registered.

Patil had been meeting someone off the books. Someone who had information worth dying for — or worth killing to keep buried.

Avinash started the engine. He did not turn toward Byculla, where Rajan Shinde waited to be fitted for another man's crime. He turned east, toward Dharavi, where the streets narrowed until headlights were useless and the only way forward was on foot, alone, in the dark, toward whatever Patil had found in that locked room's shadow.

The rain thickened. The wipers beat their idiot rhythm. In the rearview mirror, the mill's broken silhouette shrank to nothing, and with it the last clean exit Avinash Kadam would ever have.
