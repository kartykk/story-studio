Unbroken
========

Logline: A disgraced shepherd's daughter must master a forgotten magic and destroy an ancient shadow curse before her dying kingdom fades into eternal darkness.
Theme: True power is not given to the worthy — it is forged by those willing to break and rebuild themselves.

# Chapter 1: The Shepherd Who Swallowed Her Light

The ewe had gotten ideas again.

Eirys spotted her — Nell, the grey-faced matriarch with the chipped horn and the temperament of a landslide — thirty yards beyond the last living grass, standing in the place where color went to die. The old sheep's hooves rested on grey earth that had been green last week. Her jaw worked stupidly at nothing, because there was nothing there to eat. Beyond her, the world simply stopped. No horizon. No mountains. Just a wall of absence that looked like fog but smelled like the inside of a sealed tomb — cold ash and the memory of something sweet, already rotting.

"Nell." Eirys kept her voice low. Steady. The voice she used when a lamb was tangled in wire, when panic would only tighten the knot. "Come on, old terror. Nothing for you out there."

Nell's ears swiveled. She did not move.

Eirys shifted her father's crook to her left hand and walked forward, boots crunching where the grass turned brittle and grey in a line so sharp it looked drawn by a blade. The cold hit her like a held breath — not wind-cold, not ice-cold, but the cold of a room where someone has just stopped speaking and will never speak again.

Her palms prickled.

She clamped her right hand against her thigh and grabbed Nell's scruff with her left, pulling the ewe backward. Nell bleated — a thin, confused sound — and stumbled onto living grass as if waking from a dream.

"Stupid animal," Eirys muttered, but she was already checking Nell's eyes, her nostrils, the way her weight settled. The sheep's pupils were blown wide despite the dawn. Her wool — once the pale silver of the highland breed, faintly luminous in the way all silverhorn wool was, a glow so subtle you'd only notice it in full dark — looked flat. Dull. As if something had licked the light out of it.

Eirys's right palm flared.

She pressed it harder against her thigh. The heat bit through the wool of her trousers, and she felt the light trying to seep between her fingers like water through a cracked dam. She held it. Held it until her tendons ached and her breath thinned and the glow died back to the dull ember it always became if she squeezed hard enough, long enough.

Nobody saw. Nobody was up here but her and the flock — forty-three sheep where there had been sixty at shearing, and ninety the year before that. She counted them as she drove them back toward the ridge path: forty-three woolly backs in the grey half-dawn, moving in their loose river of breath and body heat. Forty-three. Enough. Not good, but enough.

The sun crested the Cradle peaks at last — a pale disc the color of old bone, more suggestion than star. It cast no shadows. Hadn't, properly, in two seasons. The light it gave was the light of a candle in a sick room: just enough to see by, never enough to feel warm.

Below and east, Caer Holm sat in its shallow valley like a cupped hand trying to hold water. Half the houses were dark. Not for lack of candles — the village still had candles, because Eirys had spent all autumn rendering embertallow from the flock's stores — but because the people who had lived in those houses were gone. Some to the inner valleys, chasing the retreating light. Some to the Dimming itself, when it moved faster than they could pack.

The lanterns along the main street burned in full daylight. They had burned all day, every day, for seven months.

Eirys brought the flock down the ridge path, her father's crook tapping the stones in the rhythm he'd taught her before she could read — *tap-tap, tap, tap-tap* — the cadence that silverhorns trusted, that settled their nerves in wind and fog. She didn't think about where she'd learned it. She didn't think about the crook's worn grip where his hand had polished the ashwood dark, or the notch at the base where he'd carved her initial when she was four.

She thought about the day. Rotate the flock to the eastern graze — still green, but barely, and she'd need to check the soil. Redistribute the candle ration now that the Haverills had left; their allotment could stretch across four households if she split it clever. Fix the stone wall on the north side where the mortar had crumbled. Talk to Eamon about his cough. Count the children.

Always count the children.

---

"Moved another ten yards in the night," said Maren, who was waiting at the gate with her hands on her walking stick and her blind eyes aimed, as always, at something no one else could see. "I felt it. Ground shook like something turned over in its sleep."

"We've time," Eirys said.

"You always say that."

"Haven't been wrong yet."

"Yet," Maren agreed, and the word sat between them with uncomfortable precision.

Eirys penned the flock and checked the water troughs — low again, the spring weakening as the blight crept closer to its source. She pulled her sleeve over her right wrist as she worked. The birthmark there was the shape of a thumbprint, slightly raised, and in full dark it glowed the faint amber of a banked coal. She had burned herself with forge-sparks deliberately at fourteen, trying to scar over it. The scars had healed. The mark hadn't.

Maren was still at the gate when Eirys came back through, as if the old woman had been waiting for her. Which she had. Maren always waited for her, because Maren remembered things the rest of the village was slowly forgetting, and remembering had made her something between an oracle and a burden.

"Sit," Maren said.

"Got the north wall to—"

"Sit, Eirys."

Eirys sat on the stone bench by the gate. The candle in the gate-lantern guttered, throwing Maren's shadow long and thin across the grey street.

"The Pell farmstead," Maren said. "Do you remember the name of their youngest? The girl with the red hair?"

Eirys opened her mouth. Closed it. She could see the girl — freckled, gap-toothed, always carrying a cat — but the name was like a word on the tip of her tongue that dissolved when she reached for it. The Pell farmstead had been swallowed six weeks ago. She had watched it vanish from this very ridge — one morning it was there, the next morning a wall of grey nothing stood where the fence had been.

"Lira," Maren said. "Her name was Lira. I can still hear it, but only just. Another week, maybe two, and it'll be gone from me too." She turned her blind face toward Eirys with terrible accuracy. "Your father said something to me once. The night before they came for him."

"Don't."

"He said power doesn't choose the worthy. It answers whoever is stubborn enough to stand in the fire and let it remake them."

Eirys stood. The bench scraped stone. "My father was branded a heretic for exactly that kind of talk. They burned our house. They burned our name. Whatever he believed about power, it didn't save him, did it?"

Maren's silence was the silence of someone who had expected exactly this and was not discouraged by it. She reached out and found Eirys's hand with the unerring precision of long affection.

"Being branded doesn't make a thing untrue, girl. It just means the truth cost something."

Eirys pulled her hand free, gently. "I've got a wall to mend."

She walked into the village with her father's crook tapping the stones and her right hand clenched in her pocket and the birthmark on her wrist pulsing with a warmth she refused to feel. Behind her, Maren sat at the gate with Lira Pell's name held in her mouth like the last ember of a fire everyone else had let go out.

---

He came at dusk — though dusk was hard to distinguish from midday now, or from dawn. Just a further dimming of the dim.

The man collapsed against the gate post as if his bones had made a collective decision to stop. He was gaunt the way drought-struck cattle get gaunt — not thin but *reduced*, as if something had been eating him from the angles inward. His clothes were shredded at the hems, bleached colorless, and he clutched a leather case to his chest the way Eirys had seen mothers clutch infants during the evacuations: with the grip that says *this or nothing*.

Eirys reached him first. She knelt, checked his breathing — shallow, rapid — and reached for his wrist to feel his pulse.

He seized her hand.

His eyes snapped open, and they were the eyes of a man who had been running from something that did not need to chase. He stared at her wrist — at the birthmark her sleeve had ridden up to expose — and his cracked lips moved.

"Lightkeeper," he said.

The word hit her like a hand across the mouth.

"You're ill," she said, pulling her wrist back. "Maren! I need water and—"

"Lightkeeper." He said it again, louder, and two villagers on the street turned to look. The word carried. It always carried. In Caer Holm, where Eirys's father had been dragged through the square by Crown soldiers while his daughter screamed, *Lightkeeper* was not a title. It was a verdict.

"Don't say that word here," Eirys hissed. She hauled him upright with the strength of someone who wrestled ewes through snowdrifts, got his arm over her shoulder, and half-carried him through the gate before he could speak again.

She put him in the empty Haverills' cottage. Fed him broth. Cleaned the blight-burns on his hands — grey patches where the skin had gone papery and numb, the mark of someone who had touched the Dimming and been touched back. He muttered through the broth about manuscripts, about a covenant, about the Unmaking, and she let him mutter the way she let a fevered sheep bleat: patiently, without investing in the content.

Then his fever broke, and he looked at her with clear eyes, and he told her the thing she had spent twelve years building a life to never hear.

"The Dimming is not weather. Not natural. It is a curse called the Unmaking, and it is accelerating because the last covenant-holder is dead." His voice was precise, measured — the voice of a man who had rehearsed this speech across miles of blighted territory. "You are the last living heir of the Lightkeeper bloodline. You are the only one who can renew the covenant. And if you do not, this kingdom will be consumed within the year."

Eirys stood. She walked to the door. She set her hand on the frame and felt the wood shudder faintly, the way everything shuddered now — a low, constant tremor, as if the ground itself were shivering.

"My father," she said, "was called a heretic and a madman for claiming our blood was special. My mother vanished twelve years ago, and I have carved a mark for every day she's been gone — four thousand, three hundred and twelve marks in the beam above my bed. I have kept this village alive with candle-wax and grazing routes and stubbornness. I am a shepherd. Find someone else."

"There is no one else."

"Then the covenant stays broken."

She left him in the cottage and walked out into the grey evening and pressed both palms flat against the cold stone wall until the light in them died.

---

The Dimming came for Caer Holm at the third hour past midnight.

Eirys felt it before she heard it — a pressure drop, like the instant before a massive storm, except the air didn't move. The silence moved. It rolled through the village like a held breath becoming a scream becoming nothing, and the lanterns on the main street guttered and went dark in sequence, *pop pop pop*, as if fingers were pinching out each flame.

She was running before she was fully awake.

The north wall — the wall she'd meant to mend — was gone. Not broken. Not collapsed. *Gone*. In its place, the Dimming poured through like smoke made of silence, and it was reaching with tendrils she could almost see — long, groping fingers of absolute dark that moved with horrible purpose toward the children's hall.

The children's hall. Where seven children slept because their families had nowhere else safe.

She could hear Tommin screaming. Tommin, who was nine, and who had woken that morning unable to remember his sister Brin's name, though Brin slept in the cot beside him. Tommin, who had stared at Brin over breakfast with the panicked eyes of someone who knows they've lost something vital and cannot even remember what it was.

The tendrils reached the hall's doorframe. The wood greyed. The candle inside went out.

Eirys stopped running. She stopped thinking. She stopped being the shepherd who swallowed her light.

She opened her hands.

It came out of her like a shout she'd been holding for twelve years — light, raw and wild and uncontrolled, the color of dawn on snow, blazing from her palms and her wrist and the spaces between her ribs as if her skeleton were a lantern and her skin the shade. The light hit the Dimming and the Dimming *screamed* — not with sound but with recoil, the tendrils snapping back like burned fingers, the wall of darkness convulsing and retreating ten, twenty, thirty yards beyond where the north wall had stood.

The cost hit her one heartbeat later.

Her knees buckled. Her vision went white, then red, then a darkness that was different from the Dimming — warmer, personal, the darkness of a body shutting down. She felt something tear loose inside her — not physical, not a muscle or a tendon, but something deeper, something that felt like a page being ripped from a book she was still reading. A memory: her mother's hand on her hair, the specific weight of it, the smell of lanolin and highland thyme. Gone. Just — gone. A space where it had been, smooth and blank as river-stone.

She hit the ground. The light died. The Dimming stayed back, cowering at the edge of its new boundary, but she could feel it gathering, patient, like an animal that had learned the shape of its prey.

Above her, the stars — what few still shone through the permanent haze — spun in slow circles.

A face appeared. Darveth. The scholar. He held her father's crook in one hand and a lamp in the other, and his expression was not surprise, not triumph, but the terrible recognition of a man seeing something he had spent decades looking for and understanding that the cost of finding it was higher than he'd calculated.

"You have no training," he said quietly. "That should have killed you."

"Might still," she managed.

"It will, if you stay here. The Dimming will come back. Harder. It has tasted you now." He knelt beside her. His voice shifted — still precise, still measured, but underneath it something cracked, something that sounded like it had been sealed for years and was leaking. "There is a place. Aeltharis. The sanctum where the first covenant was forged. The magic can only be renewed there. I know the way. I have the texts."

"I can't leave them."

"If you stay, there will be no *them* to leave. The Dimming will take Caer Holm within a fortnight. The only protection you can give these people is to go."

She lay on the cold ground with the taste of iron in her mouth and a hole in her memory where her mother's touch had been, and she thought: *This is how it happens. Not with a coronation or a prophecy. With your face in the dirt and something stolen from you that you'll never get back, and a choice that isn't really a choice at all.*

She reached for the crook. His hand was on it, and for a moment they both held the ashwood shaft — his grip careful, academic; hers blistered and sure.

She pulled it from him and used it to stand.

---

She left at first light — such as light was. Maren stood at the gate with the seven children gathered behind her, Tommin clutching Brin's hand as if afraid that letting go would make her disappear from his mind entirely. The villagers who remained watched from doorways, candles cupped in their palms, and Eirys memorized every face because she had learned in the night that memories could be taken, and she wanted to hold these as long as she could.

"You'll manage the eastern graze," she told Maren. "Water from the deep well only — spring's compromised. Candle ration holds for six weeks if you—"

"I kept this village alive before you were born, girl." Maren's blind eyes were bright. "Go."

Eirys shifted her father's crook to her shoulder. The leather pack on her back held a change of clothes, a fire kit, a ball of silverhorn wool that still held its faint glow. Darveth waited on the road beyond the gate, his manuscript case strapped across his chest, a map of blighted territory folded in his hand.

She looked back once. Caer Holm in its shallow valley. The lanterns burning in daylight. The diminished sun catching the slate rooftops and making them gleam like dull coins.

She walked through the gate and the village fell behind her and the road wound upward into grey country where the grass died by inches and the air smelled of ash and forgetting. Darveth walked beside her without speaking. Her right palm throbbed — not with pain but with presence, a low, warm pulse like a second heartbeat, as if something inside her had woken and was listening.

She pressed her hand against her thigh, but this time, she did not squeeze.
