Immolation
==========

Logline: A burned-out Mumbai detective hunting a ritualistic serial killer must expose the corrupt officials protecting him before becoming the next victim.
Theme: In a city where power and rot are indistinguishable, justice demands you become the very darkness you fight.

# Chapter 1: Ashes on the Tongue

The rain fell like it wanted to erase something.

Arvind Kadam stood at the edge of the cremation ghat with his shoes soaking through, watching the Mithi River drag itself past in a slow brown churn that smelled of sewage and marigold pulp. Four-thirty in the morning. The floodlights the first responders had brought threw white cones into the downpour, each beam alive with slanting water, and beyond their reach the city crouched in total dark — no power in this stretch of riverbank, hadn't been for months, the transformer box stripped for copper by someone who needed to eat more than they needed light.

The body lay on the concrete apron of the ghat, positioned with a care that made Arvind's stomach clench.

He had been photographing it for nine minutes. His department-issued phone was already full, so he used the Nokia he kept in his glovebox, the one with no SIM card registered to any name. The flash fired and fired. He moved around the corpse in a slow orbit, each step deliberate, his weight placed on the balls of his feet to minimize the tread pattern his regulation shoes would leave in the wet ash.

The dead man had been burned, but not the way a pyre burns a body — not the democratic, consuming fire that turns everything to the same pale powder. This burning was architectural. The torso was charred black and split along precise lines that followed the intercostal spaces between the ribs, as though someone had painted accelerant in careful stripes. The face was intact. Sandalwood paste had been applied to the forehead in three horizontal lines — Shaivite markings, tripundra, rendered with a steadiness that suggested practice or ritual conviction. Copper coins covered both eyes, the green patina of old metal bright against the ruined skin. The arms extended outward from the body, palms up, fingers curled in what Arvind recognized, after a long moment of staring, as chin mudra — thumb and forefinger touching, the gesture of received knowledge.

Someone had laid this man out like a lesson.

Arvind crouched. The rain hammered the back of his neck and ran down inside his collar. He leaned close, breathing through his mouth against the sweetness of cooked flesh underlaid with something chemical — not kerosene, something cleaner, more controlled. Beneath the sternum, visible only because the fire had cracked the skin there and pulled it back like the pages of a book, something glinted. He worked it free with the capped end of a pen. A fragment of copper, roughly triangular, one edge inscribed with marks too small to read in this light. He turned it once between his fingers, then slid it into the inner pocket of his jacket and buttoned the flap.

Behind him, four constables stood in a loose semicircle near the patrol vehicles, shifting their weight, not approaching. Two of them were smoking. One was on his phone. The fourth was Nikita Desai.

She materialized at his shoulder so quietly that he registered her presence as a change in the quality of the rain sound — a second body displacing water beside him.

"The SOC team is eleven minutes out," she said. "FJ College Road is flooded to the axle."

Arvind said nothing. He took two more photographs and straightened.

"Sir, why do you do this?"

"My job."

"Your job is to secure the scene and brief the team. Not photograph everything yourself and tell no one what you found."

He looked at her for the first time. Twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven. Regulation haircut, the short practical crop that women constables adopted not because they were told to but because the city's humidity would destroy anything longer by noon. She had good eyes — too direct for someone her rank.

"Because the moment you hand someone the truth, they decide how much of it to keep," Arvind said.

Nikita held his gaze. The rain streaked across her face, and she didn't blink. "And working alone? What does that accomplish?"

"Precision."

"The rot wins quietly, sir. That's all it accomplishes. Just — quietly."

He turned away from her and walked back toward the body, and after a beat he heard her boots retreat across the wet concrete. He didn't watch her go. He was staring at the dead man's posed hands, at the careful geometry of the burning, and behind his eyes two other photographs were surfacing — photographs pinned to the wall above his bed in the Dadar flat, photographs no one else in the department had connected because no one else had bothered to keep copies of cases that had been efficiently, institutionally forgotten.

The same mudra. The same coins. The same sandalwood lines.

Three bodies. Three altars. And no one looking but him.

---

His flat was a crime scene that no one had reported.

One room, second floor of a Dadar chawl whose stairwell smelled permanently of cooking oil and phenyl. He had lived here for three years, since Meghna left — not that she'd left, precisely; she had simply stopped returning to a place that contained him, which amounted to the same thing. He had called her four times this week. The phone rang to voicemail each time, and each time the automated voice told him the mailbox was full, and each time he set the phone down as though he'd been expecting exactly this.

The walls were papered with the dead. Photographs, photocopied FIRs, toxicology printouts, hand-drawn maps of crime scenes. He worked in layers — new cases pinned over old, the archaeology of his obsession readable in the depth of the paper. His disciplinary file sat on the kitchen counter in a manila envelope he never opened anymore. It was thicker than his solved-case record. He knew this because a clerk in the admin office had told him so last Diwali, not cruelly, just stating a fact the way you'd note the weather.

Arvind pinned the Nokia's photographs beside the two existing cases. He stepped back.

Victim one: Farooqui. Found on a Versova beach six months ago, burned with the same striped precision, coins on eyes, mudra intact. Retired clerk in the municipal water department. Acquitted of charges related to a contaminated pipeline in Malwani that hospitalized thirty children. Case collapsed when evidence went missing from the chain of custody.

Victim two: Patwardhan. Found in a Sewri dock warehouse four months ago. Same staging. Former building inspector. Linked to the structural approvals for a Ghatkopar residential complex whose stairwell collapsed during last year's monsoon, killing eight. Never charged.

Victim three, now: the ghat. He didn't have a name yet, but the constable's preliminary report mentioned a retired municipal contractor, and Arvind already knew — the way he knew things, not through logic but through the pattern-sense that operated beneath logic like a current beneath a river's surface — that this man would be connected to the Bhiwandi building collapse. The one that killed twelve. The one where the acquittal had been so swift and bloodless that even the Times of India ran only four column inches.

Three men. Three acquittals. Three pyres built with the grammar of scripture.

His phone rang. He didn't recognize the number.

---

The textile mill squatted in Lower Parel like a rotting molar — brick blackened by decades of exhaust, windows punched out, the massive chimney stack cracked down one side and colonized by pipal roots. Arvind ducked under the police tape and walked the corridor alone, his torch cutting through air that tasted of wet char and something metallic, almost medicinal.

The fourth body lay in the center of the mill floor, on a cleared circle of concrete where someone had swept away the accumulated debris of twenty years to create a clean stage. The burning here was more elaborate: the accelerant lines formed a mandala pattern across the torso, radiating outward from the navel. The limbs were arranged. The copper coins gleamed.

And beside the body, propped upright against a rusted loom frame, sat a copper plate the size of a man's palm, green-bright, incised with characters Arvind could not read but recognized instantly as Sanskrit. The lettering was small, precise, cut with a tool that left clean edges — not scratched, not improvised. Engraved.

He photographed the plate from six angles without touching it.

His DCP, Pratik Joshi, arrived at the mill within the hour, which was unusual — Joshi didn't visit crime scenes below a certain threshold of media visibility. He stood just inside the entrance and didn't approach the body.

"Gang killing," Joshi said. "Retaliatory. The slumlord angle plays. Media's already calling it street justice. Wrap it by morning."

"There's a Sanskrit inscription on a copper plate placed at the victim's feet," Arvind said.

"Gangs have consultants now. Wrap it, Kadam."

"This is the fourth."

Joshi's expression didn't change, which told Arvind everything about how much the DCP already knew. "You're on borrowed time," Joshi said. "I shouldn't have to remind you of the state of your file."

---

Sunanda Shinde answered the door in Chembur holding a steel tumbler of tea she did not offer him.

She was thinner than the last time. The chawl corridor behind her was narrow and smelled of boiling dal, and somewhere a radio played a Lata Mangeshkar song from a decade when things were presumably less ruined. Arvind stood on the threshold and did not enter, because she had not stepped aside.

"I need to ask about Pratap's case," he said.

She looked at a point three inches to the left of his face. Eight years of practice had made this almost natural for her — the geometry of a gaze calibrated to deny him the acknowledgment of eye contact while still communicating, with surgical precision, that she saw him and what she saw was rot.

"The evidence files," Arvind said. "The forensic reports that went to trial. Do you remember the pathologist's name?"

Sunanda said nothing for eleven seconds. Then: "You were the evidence, Inspector. You were the one who decided what was real."

He left with no answer and the taste of ash on his tongue that had nothing to do with the monsoon air.

---

At KEM Hospital, Dr. Yashvardhan Sathe was finishing the ghat victim's autopsy when Arvind arrived. The morgue was cool, humming with tube lights, and Sathe worked with the unhurried precision of a man performing a sacrament. He was tall, thin, silver-haired at the temples, and his hands — even gloved, even inside a body cavity — moved with a gentleness that seemed inappropriate for the work.

"Inspector Kadam. You're here about the cremation ghat gentleman." Sathe didn't look up. "Cause of death is thermal injury, extensive. Accelerant-assisted, you see, but the specifics of the accelerant will take the lab some time."

"The burn pattern is deliberate."

"Burns are chaotic by nature. I wouldn't characterize any pattern I see here as necessarily deliberate."

Arvind watched Sathe's hands. They were guiding the examination away from the intercostal precision of the burns, away from the clean edges where the fire had been applied and the untouched skin where it had not. The official report would say: extensive thermal damage, consistent with accelerant use. It would not say: someone burned this man the way a calligrapher writes — with control, with intent, with a knowledge of anatomy that made the fire legible.

"Your report on the Sewri dock victim four months ago," Arvind said. "Patwardhan. Same finding. Chaotic burns."

Sathe looked up. His eyes were dark and attentive and entirely calm. "You see, Inspector, I report what I find. If the findings across cases share characteristics, that is a question for investigation, not pathology."

"And the Versova case before that?"

"I don't recall the specifics. I perform roughly three hundred autopsies a year."

Arvind nodded. He did not say what he was thinking. He left.

---

He returned to the flat at eleven that night. The rain had not stopped. He pulled the floorboard near the bathroom and retrieved the files he kept there — photocopies made on his own money at a Matunga print shop, records of cases he was not assigned to, evidence logs he had no authorization to possess.

He spread them on the floor because the desk was already full.

Four victims. Four acquittals. Four autopsies signed by the same pathologist.

Nikita Desai had left a folded note on his desk at the station that afternoon. He'd found it when he stopped to collect his things after KEM. No name on it, just a line of text in neat handwriting: *All four victims were acquitted in cases involving deaths of unregistered migrants or informal workers. No next of kin on file for any of the deceased in the original cases.*

He stared at the note for a long time. Then he pinned it to the wall with everything else.

The connection was there, humming beneath the surface like a wire under voltage. Sathe's hands on the bodies. Sathe's reports that said nothing by saying just enough. The doctor who performed three hundred autopsies a year and couldn't recall the specifics of the one case that matched the pattern exactly.

Arvind stood in the center of his papered room and felt the familiar tightening behind his sternum — the compression that came when a case began to breathe, when the dead started telling him things the living refused to say.

He picked up the copper fragment from his jacket pocket and held it under the desk lamp. The incised marks resolved into two characters. He could not read Sanskrit, but he would learn what they said, and he would learn it without telling anyone why he needed to know.

Outside, Mumbai drowned in its own weather, and the rain said nothing, and the dead said everything, and Arvind Kadam began to build his case on a wall that only he would ever see.
